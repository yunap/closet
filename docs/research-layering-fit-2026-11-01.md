# Research: layering fit — what stylists, patternmakers and knitwear sources look at

**Status:** active — research note, 2026-11-01; source gaps filled the same day (patternmaking ease, REI, Patagonia, Singer figures, drop-shoulder designer source). No code changed. Input for an owner decision, not a ruling.

## Why this exists

The stylist keeps putting dense long-sleeved sweaters and knit tops under cardigans whose sleeves are fitted
from the elbow down, and the result is bulky and uncomfortable. Across replays the models judged fit from text
labels (`top`, `outerwear`, sleeve shape `deep_armhole` / `flared`, fit `fitted`) instead of the photos. See
[sleeve-thickness-audit-2026-10-21.md](sleeve-thickness-audit-2026-10-21.md) (owner ruling: no sleeve-thickness
field; the stylist must judge from the photographs) and [selected-piece-explore-mode-spec.md](selected-piece-explore-mode-spec.md)
§12 (the base must physically fit, including bulk, under the layer).

The question: what principles do real stylists, patternmakers and garment makers use? The owner wants reasoning a
stylist applies, not per-case rules, so this note keeps (a) the physical principles and what is looked at apart
from (b) concrete rules of thumb, and lists (c) the garment facts the principle needs.

## Source quality, honestly

Primary patternmaking material: Winifred Aldrich's *Metric Pattern Cutting for Women's Wear* (5th ed., Blackwell
2008), read in the publisher's 22-page reading sample (block-choice text and the bodice and jacket block drafts),
and the 1998 Simplicity/McCall's/Butterick/Vogue joint fit brochure (wearing ease vs design ease). The Big 4
pattern companies' ease-by-garment chart is read from a retailer's reproduction of it. The Singer upper-arm ease
figures are read in the Threads forum post that quotes them (via the Wayback Machine); the Singer book itself was
not available. REI's and Patagonia's layering pages were read from Wayback Machine snapshots. Other sources:
knitwear designers and yarn companies on ease, outdoor-clothing makers' own layering pages, two named stylists
(Angie of YouLookFab, Imogen Lamport of Inside Out Style) and an NFPA fire-safety tip.

## (a) Principles and what a stylist looks at

### 1. Ease is the room a layer has, and everything worn under it spends that room

Ease is the difference between a garment's measurement and the body's
([Lion Brand, "How much ease do I need for my garment?"](https://support.lionbrand.com/support/solutions/articles/17000035224-how-much-ease-do-i-need-for-my-garment-)).
Anything inside the outer garment uses part of that difference. Ysolda's journal names the inputs directly: how much
ease you need depends on "the thickness of the fabric, how many layers you plan to wear under the garment"
([Ysolda, "How does ease affect inclusive size ranges?", 2019](https://ysolda.com/blogs/journal/how-does-ease-affect-inclusive-size-ranges)).
Knit Picks shows it with photos: a cardigan at 1" of ease "would be challenging to layer over pieces that are not
also tight", while 10" gives "a loose fit that's perfect for layering"
([Knit Picks, "What Does Positive Ease Look Like?"](https://blog.knitpicks.com/what-does-positive-ease-look-like/)).
A woven-pattern source makes the same point at the armhole: about 2" of ease for one layer, "you might need 4
inches if you want to wear many layers underneath"
([Dresspatternmaking.com, "Ease in Patterns and Blocks"](https://dresspatternmaking.com/blog/ease-in-patterns-and-blocks)).

The pattern industry separates two kinds of ease. "Wearing ease is the minimum 'wiggle room' built into the
garment"; design ease "is the extra amount that gives the intended fashion look"
([Simplicity, McCall's, Butterick and Vogue, "Do You REALLY Know Your Pattern Size?", 1998, PDF hosted by Threads](https://images.threadsmagazine.com/app/uploads/2008/10/11135606/fitbroch.pdf)).
Patternmaking builds garments worn over others from a roomier foundation. Aldrich: "The blocks include the basic
amount of ease required for the function of the block; for example, a dress block requires less ease than a jacket
block", and "the overgarment block can be drafted to be close fitting for a formal coat or to be an easier fitting
coat". Her drafts show the step: the close-fitting bodice block is half bust plus 5 cm, the easy-fitting bodice
block half bust plus 7 cm, and the tailored jacket block half bust plus 8 cm (12 cm for easy fitting). The jacket
armhole is drafted deeper, at armscye depth plus 3 cm (5 cm), against plus 0.5 cm on the close-fitting bodice
([Aldrich, *Metric Pattern Cutting for Women's Wear*, 5th ed., Blackwell 2008, publisher reading sample, pp. 15–20](https://download.e-bookshelf.de/download/0000/5989/88/L-G-0000598988-0002339537.pdf);
read via [Wayback snapshot 2026-04-01](https://web.archive.org/web/20260401054811/https://download.e-bookshelf.de/download/0000/5989/88/L-G-0000598988-0002339537.pdf)).
Aldrich also names the fabric qualities a cutter must estimate: "weight, thickness, drape, stretch and shear"
(same sample).

**What a stylist looks at:** not "is the outer garment fitted?" but "how much room is left once the inner garment is
inside it?" That is a relation between two garments, not a property of either.

### 2. Ease is not uniform along a garment — the sleeve is judged section by section

"Different sections of a garment can have varying amounts of style ease" — a body can be oversized while the sleeves
are fitted ([Ysolda, same post](https://ysolda.com/blogs/journal/how-does-ease-affect-inclusive-size-ranges)). The
same Knit Picks comparison notes the tight-fitting example's sleeves "have negative ease where you'd typically want
positive ease" ([Knit Picks](https://blog.knitpicks.com/what-does-positive-ease-look-like/)). Tailored sleeves are
deliberately shaped this way: a jacket sleeve has fullness at the biceps and elbow and a "slight taper from elbow to
cuff" ([Proper Cloth, "How It Should Fit: Slight taper from elbow to cuff"](https://propercloth.com/reference/how-jacket-sleeve-opening-width-should-fit/)).
A dolman is defined as a sleeve "fitted at wrist but cut with a deep armhole"
([MIT apparel terms glossary, "dolman"](https://web.mit.edu/knoiva/www/Old/apparel/terms/sleeve/dolman.html)) — i.e.
a deep armhole says nothing about room at the forearm.

**What a stylist looks at:** room at the narrowest point the inner sleeve has to pass — often the forearm and wrist,
not the armhole. A label describing the top of the sleeve (`deep_armhole`) or its silhouette (`flared`) does not
answer this; the photo of the sleeve's whole length does.

### 3. Thick or textured fabric needs more room than its measurements suggest

Knitwear designer Clare Mountain-Manipon: "Heavier yarns will require more ease than finer yarns to get the same
sort of fit", and "Textured or hairy yarns like mohair also add bulk to the inside of a garment"
([Sister Mountain, "How to Use Ease When Designing Knitting Patterns"](https://clare-mountain-lphc.squarespace.com/blog/ease-knitting-pattern-design)).
The same logic applies to the inner garment: its thickness is the bulk the outer one must hold. Gauge is the knit's
measure of this — "the smaller the gauge number, the heavier the knit", with 12-gauge described as a "good weight
for layering" ([Pringle of Scotland, "All about gauge"](https://pringlescotland.com/pages/all-about-gauge)). Among cut
fabrics, double knits (ponte, interlock) feel "firm and thick", have "a lot of body compared to other knits" and are
"less stretchy"; interlock is "made with two layers of jersey"
([Seamwork, "Working with Double Knit"](https://www.seamwork.com/articles/working-with-double-knit)). Less stretch
means the outer sleeve cannot borrow give from the inner one.

**What a stylist looks at:** how thick and how stiff the inner sleeve is (chunky vs fine knit, double knit vs fine
jersey), judged from the photo, not from the category word "top" or "sweater".

### 4. Construction decides where the extra fabric sits

Knitwear designer Elizabeth Smith explains why drop shoulders carry fabric under the arm. Raglan and set-in sleeves
have angled shaping that lets the arm move; a drop-shoulder armhole has none, so the body needs extra width there.
Drop-shoulder patterns therefore often suggest 6–12" of positive ease, against "the 1-4″ range" common for raglans.
With less ease, "Your sleeve/arm opening will fall higher-up on the arm", and the right amount depends on whether
layers are worn under or over the garment
([Elizabeth Smith Knits, "Drop-Shoulder Ease", 2021, updated 2023](https://elizabethsmithknits.com/2021/10/15/drop-shoulder-ease/)).
A university extension guide gives the woven equivalent: a kimono sleeve is cut with the bodice, so "The shape and
width of the sleeve will determine its comfort"; a narrow, close-sloping one may need a gusset; a shirt-style sleeve
"fits more loosely under the arm than a plain set-in sleeve"
([New Mexico State University Cooperative Extension, "Sewing Sleeves", Guide C-211, revised 2015](https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_c/C211.pdf)).
A YouLookFab reader with structured short-sleeved tops found her jackets' narrow sleeves
"won't go over them"; stylist Angie's answer was a different kind of outer layer, not a different rule: "You need a
cape/poncho/kimono style topper"
([YouLookFab forum, "The search for a loose sleeved topper"](https://youlookfab.com/welookfab/topic/the-search-for-a-loose-sleeved-topper---ideas)).

**What a stylist looks at:** where each garment carries its volume (shoulder, underarm, forearm, cuff) and whether
the inner garment's volume lands where the outer one has room.

### 5. Layers are built from the body outward, closest-fitting first

The outdoor makers state this as a system, not a styling opinion. Arc'teryx: base layers "should fit snugly, so you
can add an insulating layer and shell without losing freedom of movement", and "A shell will be your outermost
layer" ([Arc'teryx, "Layering Clothes Like a Pro"](https://arcteryx.com/product-advice/layering-clothes-like-a-pro)).
Montane: base layer "worn closest to the skin", midlayer "worn over the base layer and under the outer shell"
([Montane, "The Outdoor Layering System"](https://montane.com/pages/the-layering-system)). Mont (Australia) writes it
into its fit names: "Next to Skin Fit … Body hugging", "Regular Fit … Designed to fit over base and mid layers without
restricting movement", "Relaxed Fit … Cut loose enough for layering", and insulation should be sized to allow "a base
layer and lightweight mid layer underneath" ([Mont, "Sizing charts"](https://www.mont.com.au/pages/sizing-charts)).
Patagonia gives the same order. Baselayers are "your sweat-wicking layer for next-to-skin comfort" and should "sit
comfortably against your skin". "You wear your midlayer between your baselayer and your shell", and the midlayer
comes as "jackets, vests, half-zips". The shell is "worn over your midlayer", and "You might not wear a shell all day
long". Patagonia also says to choose the baselayer with the midlayer's warmth in mind, not on its own
([Patagonia, "How to Layer for Cold Weather"](https://www.patagonia.com/guides/cold-weather-layering/), read via
[Wayback snapshot 2026-01-25](https://web.archive.org/web/20260125210813/https://www.patagonia.com/guides/cold-weather-layering/)).
Imogen Lamport (image consultant): "Layering starts closest to your skin"
([Inside Out Style, "How to wear layers in winter", 2017](https://insideoutstyleblog.com/2017/08/how-to-wear-layers-winter.html)).
Style editor Lois Joy Johnson calls it a "clothes sandwich": "the heaviest pieces on top, lightest on bottom"
([AARP, "How to dress warm without bulk", 2023](https://www.aarp.org/entertainment/style-trends/info-2023/how-to-dress-warm-without-bulk)).

So "thinnest / closest to the body first" does exist in authoritative sources — most precisely in the makers'
own fit categories, where each layer is *cut* for what goes under it. The fashion sources state it more loosely.

### 6. The thinnest layer is the one that touches the sleeve

Stylists who write in their own words consistently reach for fine knits when something must go under something.
Angie (YouLookFab): "I find it hard to layer a chunky knit underneath a jacket or coat", and "the perfect long sleeved
layering T is crucial" ([YouLookFab, "How to wear chunky knits", 2007](https://youlookfab.com/2007/12/21/how-to-wear-chunky-knits/)).
Imogen Lamport: you can layer "multiple fine knits made from wool or cashmere", because fibres like cashmere "are
very warm but can still be lightweight and fine"
([Inside Out Style, "How to stay warm in winter without the bulk", 2010](https://insideoutstyleblog.com/2010/03/how-to-stay-warm-in-winter-without-the-bulk.html)).
Johnson's sweater-vest-over-base-under-jacket keeps warmth on the torso "without adding bulk to the arms"
([AARP](https://www.aarp.org/entertainment/style-trends/info-2023/how-to-dress-warm-without-bulk)) — the sleeve
is where bulk is least tolerated, so warmth goes where there is room.

The historic twinset is this principle as a product: a cardigan sold with a matching **sleeveless or short-sleeved**
pullover to wear under it ([Dictionary.com, "twinset"](https://www.dictionary.com/browse/twinset); credited to
Otto Weisz at Pringle in the 1930s per the
[V&A collections entry](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1194286), read via the V&A collections API).

### 7. Whether a layer is needed is a function question, asked about each part of the day

The makers frame layering as adjustment, not decoration: "Too hot? Try removing your hood, unzipping your pits, or
removing a layer altogether", and pay attention to how you feel as you move
([Arc'teryx](https://arcteryx.com/product-advice/layering-clothes-like-a-pro)). REI: layering "lets you regulate
comfort by slipping layers on and off as your activity level or the weather changes". "You can peel off layers if
things heat up, but you can't put on layers that you didn't bring along". "If the rain and wind let up, remove your
shell. If hiking alone isn't warming you up, add a middle layer". REI also warns that advice "based solely on
weather" overlooks "exertion level and personal metabolism"
([REI Expert Advice, "Layering Basics"](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/layering-basics.html), read via
[Wayback snapshot 2026-10-04](https://web.archive.org/web/20261004151551/https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/layering-basics.html)).
For a stove, the
authority is fire safety, not style: NFPA advises to "avoid wearing loose clothing or dangling sleeves while
cooking" ([ISHN, "NFPA offers cooking safety tips for the Thanksgiving holiday", 2008](https://www.ishn.com/articles/87803-nfpa-offers-cooking-safety-tips-for-the-thanksgiving-holiday-11-25));
NFPA-branded handouts hosted by local agencies say "wear short, close-fitting or tightly rolled sleeves" because
loose clothing "can dangle onto stove burners and can catch fire if it comes in contact with a gas flame or electric burner"
([NFPA, "Cooking Safety: Checklist", PDF hosted at rvcfire.org](https://www.rvcfire.org/uploads/pdf/household-handouts/Cooking_Safety_Checklist.pdf)).

**What a stylist looks at:** what the person physically does in each part of the day (standing at a hob; walking for
twenty minutes at 50–65°F), whether the layer serves that part, and whether it comes off and goes back on easily.
A layer worn only for the walk is the removable one; the piece worn at the stove is the one whose sleeves matter.
No source found gives a temperature threshold for a short walk; that remains judgment.

## (c) Garment facts a stylist needs to apply the principle

From the principles above, the judgment needs, for the **pair**:

1. **Inner garment's sleeve:** present or not, length (sleeveless / short / long), and thickness and stiffness —
   fine jersey vs double knit, fine-gauge vs chunky knit, smooth vs textured/hairy.
2. **Outer garment's sleeve along its length:** room at the armhole, upper arm, forearm and wrist separately; whether
   it tapers from the elbow; whether it is lined (slides) or knit (grips).
3. **Outer garment's stretch:** a stretchy knit cardigan forgives some inner bulk; a stable woven or double knit does not.
4. **Where each garment carries volume** (drop shoulder, dolman underarm, cuff) — do the volumes collide?
5. **The activity per part of the day** (stove, walking, sitting) and whether the layer is meant to come off.

Most of these are visible in a photo of each garment; few are captured by the current text labels. `deep_armhole`
describes one section of the sleeve; `fitted` describes the body or the whole; neither says what is at the forearm,
and neither says how thick the inner sleeve is.

## What this suggests for the app (tentative)

- The evidence supports what the owner already ruled: fit under a layer is a **relation between two garments**,
  judged section by section along the sleeve and against the inner fabric's thickness. A single-garment label cannot
  carry it, which is consistent with the replays where models trusted `deep_armhole`/`fitted` and got bulk.
- The principle a prompt could state, as reasoning rather than a case list: *before putting one garment under
  another, look at both photos and check the outer sleeve has room for the inner sleeve all the way to the wrist,
  given how thick the inner fabric is.* This is the "ease is spent by what is inside" principle, not a ban on
  sweaters under cardigans — sources show fine knits and sleeveless/short-sleeved bases are the usual answer, and
  a roomy-sleeved outer layer is the other.
- Sources consistently treat warmth placement and removability as part of the decision (vest instead of a second
  sleeve; the walk layer is the removable one; close or rolled sleeves at a stove). That fits the
  [situation-fit-plan.md](situation-fit-plan.md) direction of reasoning per part of the day.
- Patternmaking practice agrees with the outdoor makers: a garment meant to go over others is drafted from a
  roomier block with a deeper armhole (Aldrich; the Big 4 chart's separate jacket and coat columns). So whether an
  outer garment was *cut* to go over things is itself a garment fact, and it shows in the photo (shoulder line,
  armhole depth, sleeve width). Category alone does not tell you: a cardigan can be cut close like a bodice.
- Not supported by this research: any numeric threshold the app could apply from tags. The numbers above are
  maker/designer guidance for drafting, and the app does not know body measurements or garment ease. Whether the
  models actually look at the sleeve in the photos when told to is an empirical question for an offline replay, not
  something these sources answer.
